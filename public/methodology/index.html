Overview

Unlock The Count is an election data website for every level. That means candidates, polls, races, forecasts and vote tracking.

I built it because most forecasts hand you a number and ask you to trust it. You rarely see the rules behind it. You almost never see what moved it. I wanted the opposite. Every number on this site comes from a written rule. Every rule has a weight. Every input has a date and a source.

This page explains all of it. It covers the data, the math, the charts and the colors. It also lists every place where I made a judgment call instead of using hard data. If something on the site isn't explained here, that's a mistake on my part, and I want to hear about it.

I'm Jayson Frascatore. I'm a data and quality assurance analyst. I worked on the polling averages and forecast models that The Hill and NewsNation relied on at Decision Desk HQ. I write about politics, and I build these tools in Python and HTML.

Polls

The site doesn't treat every poll the same. Each pollster gets a grade from a published rating, and the grade sets how much its poll counts.

A counts 1.00B counts 0.85C counts 0.65D counts 0.25No grade counts 0.25

Two more rules keep one pollster from taking over. First, only each pollster's latest poll counts. Second, a poll drops out of the average 30 days after it leaves the field.

The average for each candidate is a weighted average of their share across those polls. Candidates listed as "undecided," "other" or "someone else" are left out of the named field.

Primary questions are only asked of each party's own voters. That group is smaller than the full sample, so the margin of error on a primary number is larger than the one printed for the whole poll.

Probability

Presidential Probability is the first half of Candidates. It answers one question: will this person declare a candidacy for president in 2028? The answer is a probability from 0% to 100%.

I chose a probability on purpose. A score from 0 to 100 can't be checked. A probability can. If the site puts ten people at 30%, about three of them should declare. When the field is set, every call will be graded in public.

Everyone starts at 25%. That's the starting line before any evidence. From there, the Decision Maze adds or subtracts points, and the points turn into the probability. A formal declaration sets the chance to 100%. A binding promise not to run sets it to 3%. Dropping out sets it to 0%.

Nothing is added up into a single score. Each card and profile lists every input with its weight and the candidate's reading, one by one.

Charts

Every trend line uses the same fixed scale from 0% to 100%. Nothing is stretched to fit, so a flat line near 30% looks low because it is low. Each line is that candidate's own chance, so two candidates in the same party can both be high. Lines use each candidate's own color with a light outline so dark colors stay visible.

Maze

The Decision Maze is the engine behind Presidential Probability. It's a set of rings. The inner rings are small, local signals. The outer rings are big, national ones. The exit is the announcement.

Every signal sits on a ring and has a weight. Each signal also gets a reading from −1 to +1. The points for that signal are its weight times its reading. A candidate who won their own midterm race gets a reading of 1 on the midterms ring, which is worth 1.50 points.

Government Ranking

Current office and former office read the same ladder: Vice President 1.00, Cabinet 0.85, Senator 0.75, Governor 0.70, House 0.45, Mayor 0.30, never elected 0. The current office counts at full weight (1.00). A former office counts at half weight (0.50), and only when it ranks above the job they hold now. A former vice president who holds no office today reads 0 on current office and 1.00 on former office.

Last election

Their last general election before 2026: won by 10 or more reads +1, won by less reads +0.5, lost by less than 10 reads −0.5, lost by 10 or more reads −1. Anyone who was on the 2026 ballot is scored by the 2026 midterms instead, so the same race never counts twice.

Kinds

Signals come in two kinds. Intent signals show whether someone is getting ready to run. Strength signals show whether they could win. Intent counts two and a half times as much as strength. A trip to New Hampshire says more about running than a good poll does.

The math works on a scale called log odds. You don't need to know it to read the site. This table shows what intent points do to someone starting at 25%.

The curve flattens near 0% and 100%. No single signal can take someone all the way to either end. Only a declaration, a binding promise or dropping out does that.

Rules

Candidates repeat themselves, and they change their minds. Four rules handle that.

  1. Repeating still counts, but less each time. The strongest statement counts in full. The next strongest counts half. The one after that counts a quarter, and so on.
  2. A change of heart wipes the slate. If someone says no four times and then says "I'm considering it," the four no's stop counting.
  3. No comment changes nothing. It still goes in the log.
  4. Reaffirming repeats the last position. "My thinking has not changed" counts as saying the same thing again.

Example

Jon Ossoff shows how this works with real statements. He started at 35.47% on June 1.

Each denial counted for less than the one before it. The pledge in October counted the most, so it moved to the front of the line. If Ossoff said "my thinking has not changed" today, he would drop to 13.31%.

Simulations

The maze gives each candidate a chance. Then the site runs that chance 11,111 times. In each run, a random number is drawn. If it lands under the chance, the candidate declares in that run. If not, they don't.

The share of runs where they declare is the number on the card, and the card says how many runs that was. "7,858 of 11,111 simulations" means 7,858 runs where Kamala Harris declared and 3,253 where she didn't, or 70.7%.

The random numbers are seeded by the candidate's name, so the same inputs always give the same count. Anyone can rerun them and get the same answer. The profile shows the runs as 100 squares: green for yes, red for no. Each square is about 111 runs.

These odds reflect the site's stated weights. They are not observed base rates. Nobody has measured how often a person in each situation goes on to run, so the weights are my judgment, written down before the data they score.

Cards

Every candidate card uses the same template. On a computer the page shows five cards, with the two on each side fading out. On a phone it shows three.

  • The tag in the top corner is their phase: Watching, Contender, Candidate or Not running.
  • Presidential Probability is the chance to declare a candidacy, with the number of simulations behind it and the change since the start of the trend.
  • The bar is in the candidate's color, on a scale from 0% to 100%.
  • The trend covers the last 60 days on a fixed 0% to 100% scale.
  • Primary average is their share in the weighted polling average.
  • View Profile opens their full profile. Viability Market opens that candidate's market.

The profile shows the trend with each move numbered, a list of those moves with what caused them, the simulation squares, every input with its weight and their reading, and the sources. Inputs are never totaled into a score.

Viability Market

The Viability Market is the second half of Candidates: unlocking the path to the nomination. Each candidate's market opens the day they declare. It follows their odds in the early states, past Super Tuesday, in the delegate count and to the nomination. Every candidate has a link of their own, like /candidates/kamala-harris/market.

Before a candidate declares, their market shows a forecast instead: where they would likely poll in the primary if they ran. The sentence reads, for example, "If Kamala Harris announces a presidential run, Harris is forecasted to poll as a potential frontrunner in the primary, based on weighted inputs and models."

Primaries

Once candidates declare, the Viability Market takes over. The Primary Path in Tools plays out the primaries 11,111 times. Each simulation goes through five steps.

  1. Who runs. Declared candidates always run. Everyone else runs at their Presidential Probability. Anyone who doesn't run hands their supporters to the candidates closest to them.
  2. Polling error. Every candidate's real support can differ from the polls. The farther away the first vote, the bigger the gap. A year out, a candidate's real support could be less than half or more than double what the polls show today.
  3. The early states. They vote in date order. Each state has its own voters. A candidate gets a boost in their home state and a smaller one in their home region. A state's voters also lean toward candidates whose coalition matches theirs. The winner of each state gets about a 16% bounce. Anyone under 10% loses about 10% of their support.
  4. Super Tuesday. All of its states vote on the same day. Then the field shrinks.
  5. The rest of the calendar. The remaining states vote as one group. The candidate with the most delegates is the nominee.

Dropouts

Candidates leave the race in the simulations the way they do in real life. After each early state, anyone under 8% twice in a row drops out 60% of the time. From the third contest on, anyone under 5% does the same. After Super Tuesday, anyone who won no state there and took under 20% drops out 80% of the time.

When someone drops out, their voters move. Sixty percent go to the candidates closest to them on the lane map. The other forty percent split by size, the way the field as a whole would. Voters only move in states that haven't voted yet.

The lane map is my estimate of where each candidate's coalition sits. For Democrats, it covers progressive voters, Black voters, Latino voters, working class voters and the party establishment. For Republicans, it covers the MAGA base, evangelical voters, the establishment and libertarian voters. Each candidate gets a score from −1 to +1 on each one.

The dropout panel shows three things for any candidate you pick. It shows where their voters would go, who they would most likely endorse, and how the race changes if they left today. The endorsement odds include a 35% chance they don't endorse anyone right away.

Map

Each state on the Primary Path map is colored by the candidate most likely to win it. A darker color means a safer lead. The gold numbered circles show the early states in voting order. The small labels mark Super Tuesday states. Gray states vote later. Clicking a state shows every candidate's chance there.

The bars have two views. "Chance to win" counts simulations where a candidate didn't run as losses, so each state adds up to 100%. "If they run" only counts the simulations where they entered the race.

Calendar

The Democratic order was approved in August 2026. South Carolina votes January 22, 2028, and Nevada votes February 1. New Hampshire, New Mexico, Michigan and Virginia follow in February, but their exact dates aren't set. The site uses placeholder dates for them, and every placeholder is marked with a star. The Republican calendar isn't set yet. The site uses the expected order of Iowa, New Hampshire, Nevada and South Carolina. Super Tuesday is March 7, 2028. Its states are based on 2024 state laws.

Attention

The Attention Index tells you how much to watch each candidate. It mixes three things. Their chance of being the nominee counts 40%. Their chance of getting past Super Tuesday counts 40%. Their chance of winning at least one early state counts 20%. That total is multiplied by their chance of running. Each party's Attention Index adds up to 100.

Forecast

The general election forecast is on Matchups, under Electoral Analysis. It also runs 11,111 simulations. It builds every state's forecast in five steps.

  1. The national environment. I start with the national result from each recent election, weighted by how recent it is.
  2. Factors. Things like approval and the economy move the environment.
  3. Polling. General election polls are blended in by grade.
  4. Two years out. The result is pulled part of the way back toward a tie.
  5. State lean. Each state's lean is added to the national number.

Each factor's reasoning is written out on the forecast page. For example, approval is based on Reuters and Ipsos having Trump at 32% in November 2026.

Polling

Two years out, general election polls predict very little. So they count for at most 25% of the national number, and less for lower graded pollsters.

Tie

Big leads two years out tend to shrink by Election Day. So the forecast keeps 60% of the national lead and pulls the rest back toward a tie.

States

Each state's lean is how much more Democratic or Republican it voted than the country. I use 2020 and 2024, with 2024 counting three times as much. A state's forecast margin is its lean plus the national number. Maine and Nebraska split their electoral votes by district, so their districts are forecast separately.

Error

Each simulation lets the national number miss by a random amount, usually within about 5 points either way. Each state can also miss on its own by about 3 points. The national miss moves every state together. That's why states tend to swing as a group.

Trackers

Trackers follow one fight at a time, where the outcome comes down to a count. A tracker starts when a major political event needs a tally. That could be a leadership race, major legislation, a potential impeachment, calls to resign, or an expulsion.

Every tracker is built for its own fight. There's no single weight that applies to all of them. Each one explains its own rules, its own sources and its own math on its page.

The first is The 29th Vote. It tracks which Senate Democrats publicly back Chuck Schumer for Majority Leader. It takes 29 of the 56 votes to win.

Debates

The Debate Impact Score measures how much a debate helped or hurt each candidate. It compares the week before a debate with the week after. Polling change counts 40%. The change in their Viability Market (their nomination odds) counts 25%. Fundraising after the debate counts 25%. Favorability change counts 10%.

There haven't been any 2028 debates yet. The Authentication Key tab uses sample candidates labeled A through F to show the layout. Those numbers are not real, and the page says so.

History

Past numbers are never rewritten. Each day's chance of running is saved once and stays saved, even if a weight changes later. The same goes for the Trackers.

Every saved day records which version of the method made it. If I ever change a weight, the version number changes too. You can always tell which rules produced which day.

Some history was rebuilt after the fact. Presidential Probability from June through October 2026 was rebuilt from dated statements, because daily tracking started on November 1. Every chart marks that stretch as rebuilt.

Estimates

These are the places where I made a judgment call instead of using measured data. I'm listing all of them so you can weigh them yourself.

  • Each candidate's standing in the maze, like "national figure" or "won their own midterm race."
  • The Government Ranking ladder and the last election readings.
  • The starting point of 25% for everyone in Presidential Probability.
  • The Viability Market forecast readings: name recognition, coalition backing, previous nomination and Standing Point.
  • The readings on the Statement Scale.
  • The impact value given to big news events.
  • The announcement calendar.
  • The lane map, and how each state's primary voters line up.
  • Delegate counts for each state, which are based on 2024.
  • Placeholder dates for New Hampshire, New Mexico, Michigan, Virginia and the whole Republican calendar.
  • The dropout rules and the 60 and 40 split for where voters go.
  • The points and confidence weights for each general election factor.
  • Past state results and the midterm history table, which are rounded.
  • The party starting points in the Trackers.

Every one of these lives in a single settings file. I wrote them down before scoring the data, and they only change on purpose.

Limits

Here's what the site can't do yet, and where you should be careful.

  • One primary poll is a thin base. A single new poll can move the averages and the forecasts a lot.
  • Fundraising and favorability don't count yet. They will once real numbers come in.
  • Polls this early mostly measure name recognition. That's why polling is one signal among many and not the whole story.
  • The simulations treat each candidate's decision to run as separate. In real life, one candidate's choice affects another's.
  • Probabilities are not predictions of certainty. A 20% chance should happen about one time in five.

Updates

The site runs on plain files. When a new poll, statement or vote is added, every number recalculates the next time the page loads.

Behind the site, a Python version of the model runs every morning. It does three jobs. It checks that the Python version and the website give exactly the same answers. It runs the primary simulations. It saves each day to the history files.

Every run goes to a test version first. Nothing reaches the live site until it passes all of the checks. The "Last updated" line at the top of every page shows when the data last changed.

Ads

Unlock The Count runs ads through Google AdSense to cover its costs. Ads never affect any number, rating or forecast on the site. No campaign, party or group pays for coverage. If the site ever takes a sponsor, it will say so clearly on the page.

Corrections

If you find a mistake, I want to know. That includes a wrong number, a broken link, or a statement read the wrong way. Every correction is made in the open, and the history files show when it was made.
